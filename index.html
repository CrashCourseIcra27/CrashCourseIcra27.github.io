Abstract

Emergency-driving competence depends both on learning effective responses and on obtaining demonstrations that a sensor-based policy can use. We study these two questions in CrashCourse, a crash-grounded closed-loop simulation suite. First, we train a privileged driving teacher with MU-SAC, using distributional critics, multiplicative steering conditioning and uncertainty-weighted replay; the teacher learns by reinforcement without a behavior-cloning initialization or reference policy. Second, we construct a small dataset of synchronized sensor observations and emergency-response targets from its training-side rollouts, and adapt off-the-shelf driving models by supervised fine-tuning of added adapters while their original weights stay frozen. Matched teacher ablations test the RL method, while nominal-only adaptation, data-source controls and encounter-budget sweeps test the value of the CrashCourse demonstrations. Ordinary-driving evaluation measures regression rather than assuming that frozen base weights prevent it.

Teacher to student

MU-SAC trains the privileged teacher in the reactive CrashCourse environment. A frozen checkpoint is rolled out on the training split only, and each accepted encounter is exported with synchronized student sensors, causal history and native targets. Released sensor policies are then adapted by supervised fine-tuning of added adapters, with their original weights frozen. Privileged simulator state never crosses into the deployed student: what transfers is observations and teacher actions.

The benchmark

CrashCourse scenarios are built from real crash geometries and replayed in closed loop with reactive adversaries: an adversary tracks the ego vehicle every frame to preserve the intended conflict geometry, follows explicit velocity control once the ego crosses the trigger, and returns to simulator autopilot afterwards, so the conflict is reproducible without the aftermath being scripted. The scenario families, towns and conditions are listed in the appendix material below.

Two measurements separate it from the closest broad benchmark: 33% of CrashCourse's actor interactions leave the ego under two seconds to react, against 14% of Bench2Drive's, and the impacts move to the front of the vehicle — 65% frontal against 13%. Bench2Drive penalizes a policy for being struck while it hesitates; CrashCourse penalizes it for driving into a conflict it cannot resolve.

Code, data and video

The CrashCourse scenario suite and evaluation harness, the teacher training code, the exported emergency demonstrations and the qualitative video are released here once the runs they document are complete. S1CARLA Benchmark Scenarios

Family names, towns and coverage below were checked against the executed corpus; the individual speeds and distances quoted are from the scenario specifications and have not been re-measured from logs.

Corpus and split. The evaluated corpus is 164 qualified scenario instances across ten CARLA towns, 82 for training and 82 for test, under ten weather presets. Table S1 gives the composition town by town and preset by preset. The towns are not sampled evenly, and deliberately so: the highway maps Town04, Town12 and Town13 carry half the corpus because most of the families are high-speed conflicts that need the room, Town06 and Town03 carry the multi-lane and junction cases, and Town01, Town05, Town07, Town11 and Town15 appear where a single family needed a specific piece of road geometry. The presets cover clear, cloudy, wet and rainy conditions at noon, sunset and night; they change lighting and visibility only. Grip is not a weather effect here: in CARLA's route mode the wet and icy-looking weather fields leave the tyre friction model untouched, so the grip-degraded routes are the ones whose scenario code installs an explicit friction trigger. The 45 scenario families are replicated across towns, so families are assigned whole to one side of the split rather than drawn at random. The six dimensions were labelled by reading each family's scenario source rather than by matching names.

Designing highly dynamic collision scenarios in CARLA is challenging. In general, we trigger a scenario based on a position threshold. While a policy could, in principle, drive cautiously (e.g., at half the speed limit), we vary the distance and speed of the events such that a collision could still occur. We also incorporate surrounding traffic at the speed limit, such that overly abrupt braking or slowness can also induce collision risk (i.e., from behind). Qualitative examples from our scenarios are shown in our supplementary video.

Highway and High-Speed Conflicts. High-speed environments demand rapid perception and control from state-of-the-art models due to high kinetic energy and drastically reduced reaction windows. We evaluate agent resilience across distinct test cases implemented primarily in Town04 and Town06, with the replicated families extending into Town12 and Town13. In the adversarial breach scenario, a vehicle overtakes and executes a 7.5m/s lateral slide into the ego lane before braking to a halt, boxing the ego vehicle against a slow-moving 80km/h lead truck. Similarly, the highway cut-across and adjacent lane spin scenarios force the ego vehicle to handle sudden PIT-like intrusions and spinning dynamics from neighboring lanes that motivate a tested braking-versus-steering comparison. To test sustained obstacle detection, our highway ADAS obstacle suite and freeway crash scene variants present agents with late-revealed stationary wrecks, debris, and fleeing pedestrians.

Traffic Traps. Heavy commercial vehicles introduce unique visual occlusions and sudden lateral threats that break the nominal tracking assumptions of baseline models. The truck sudden lane change scenario places a large vehicle in the adjacent lane that blindly merges right at 4.5m/s, occupying the ego vehicle's right zone and constraining the available lateral gaps. We also evaluate longitudinal prediction capabilities through the blind spot trap, where a heavy truck brakes hard directly ahead while an adversarial vehicle pacing at 55km/h simultaneously blocks the left lane. Furthermore, high-velocity geometric edge cases are evaluated via the head-on intruder family, replicated in Town01, Town12 and Town13, where a vehicle crosses into the ego's carriageway and approaches head-on, forcing the planner to rapidly update its same-direction priors under an extreme closing rate.

Urban Intersections and Cross-Traffic. Urban junctions introduce complex semantic geometries and unpredictable cross-traffic trajectories that challenge an agent's ability to safely navigate contested spaces. Our benchmark includes specific intersection scenarios located across Town03, Town12, and Town13 to test right-of-way violations and dense traffic maneuvers. In the classic red-light runner scenario, the ego vehicle enters a junction on a green signal at 25km/h while a cross-traffic actor violates the light at 60km/h, creating a short-warning broadside conflict. We adapt this for asymmetric geometry in the Texas left-turn variant, forcing the ego vehicle to pause its committed turn when an oncoming vehicle runs a red light across a wide multi-lane arterial.

Aggressive Turning. Small actors and aggressive turning kinematics require specialized spatial reasoning capabilities to prevent catastrophic side impacts. The motorcycle hanging scenario launches a fast-moving rider at 90km/h straight into the path of an ego vehicle committed to a slow left turn, testing collision kinematics that end with the motorcycle lodged on overhead signal structures. Additionally, the Town13 crosswalk cut-in evaluates lateral robustness against an actor that starts behind the ego vehicle, overtakes in the adjacent lane and sharply cuts right into the ego path to execute an immediate right turn, and the lane-filtering scenarios in Town04 and Town15 put a rider between lanes alongside the ego.

Residential Occlusions and Vulnerable Road Users. Residential and curbside environments obscure critical hazards behind static infrastructure, forcing the ego vehicle to anticipate sudden intrusions from completely occluded sightlines. We examine these occluded encounters through scenarios centered on short-warning lateral crossings. The hidden pedestrian and mid-block school drop-off scenarios hide vulnerable road users behind parked vans and queued traffic, requiring the ego vehicle to safely modulate its 15mph speed before the pedestrian visibly breaches the lane boundary. We similarly test the sudden loss of balance in the cyclist fall scenario, where an adjacent rider collapses sideways into the vehicle path at extremely close range.

Vehicle Failures, Debris and Projectiles. The largest group of families does not involve another driver's mistake at all: the hazard is the road or the vehicle itself, and it is these families that are replicated across Town06, Town12 and Town13. Tyre blowouts and a tyre explosion destabilize the ego at speed; a loss-of-control family removes its authority outright. Debris fields, a rolling debris family and a runaway wheel projectile put unmodelled objects in the lane at closing speed, while the underblast families and a rig hazard release material from a vehicle ahead. Barrier rebound sends the ego back into traffic after a first contact, which is the secondary-collision case: the evaluated hazard is what follows the initial impact, not the impact itself.

Adversarial Traffic and Sudden Reveals. A second group scripts a hostile or failing driver. A rear aggressor pressures the ego from behind and a resting pursuer waits ahead of it; twin overtake convergence closes both adjacent lanes at once; close lateral encroachment and lane intrusion squeeze the ego's lane without a full merge; a forced merge pit rebound and a following pileup trigger commit contact and leave the aftermath to the simulator. Sudden reveals form the rest: a lead vehicle swerves to expose a hard-braking vehicle it had occluded, a drive-then-stop family halts a leader without warning, and a highway gore cutoff cuts across the ego at a diverging exit. A driver-medical-drift family has the leading vehicle simply stop steering. An icy-road twin slide is the low-grip case, and is one of the families whose scenario code installs a real friction trigger rather than a wet-looking sky.

Stationary-to-Dynamic Transitions. Parked vehicles and static roadside assets frequently transition into active dynamic threats with minimal pre-motion warnings. The door open scenario evaluates an agent's reaction to a sudden lane intrusion from a tightly parked curbside vehicle, providing almost zero longitudinal warning distance. Furthermore, the vehicle reverse and side street wall occlusion variants place hidden actors in residential driveways who execute decisive, high-acceleration pull-outs directly into the ego lane. These scenarios test responses to an off-road or stationary actor entering the active lane with limited visible warning. Information, anticipation, and control explanations require paired analyses.

S2Implementation and Reproducibility

Teacher critic and replay. Each ensemble critic predicts return quantiles $q_k^i(z,a)$ and their mean $\bar q_k(z,a)$. With a sampled target-critic subset $\mathcal K$, the target is

\begin{align} k^*&=\arg\min_{k\in\mathcal K}\bar q_k(z',a'),\\ y_j&=r+\gamma(1-d)\big[q_{k^*}^j(z',a')-\alpha\log\pi_\theta(a'\mid z')\big]. \label{eq:target} \end{align}

Here $a'$ is sampled from the current actor and $d$ denotes terminal failure or completion under the frozen bootstrap rule. Quantile critics minimize

\begin{equation} L_k=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N \rho_{\tau_i}^{\kappa}\big(y_j-q_k^i(z,a)\big)\right], \label{eq:quantile} \end{equation}

where $\tau_i=(i-0.5)/N$ and $\rho$ is the quantile-Huber loss. The teacher actor is optimized against these critics and entropy without the student paper's BC-reference regularizer.

MU-SAC conditioning and replay. MU-SAC projects the state and an encoded continuous steering action into a shared width $c$, then fuses them multiplicatively before the quantile heads:

\begin{equation} x=\frac{h_{\rm state}(z)\odot h_{\rm cond}(a_{\rm steer})}{\sqrt c}. \label{eq:film} \end{equation}

The heads represent the discrete acceleration actions. The ensemble supplies between-critic disagreement and average within-critic spread,

\begin{align} \sigma^2_{\rm epi}&=\operatorname{Var}_k(\bar q_k(z,a)),\\ \sigma^2_{\rm ale}&=\mathbb{E}_k[\operatorname{Var}_i(q_k^i(z,a))],\\ U(z,a)&=\sigma^2_{\rm epi}/\sigma^2_{\rm ale}. \label{eq:unc} \end{align}

This ratio drives uncertainty-weighted replay. These quantities are operational proxies, not a proven decomposition of uncertainty. The executed settings are 32 quantiles, 10 critics, a 16-dimensional steering lift and $c=256$, with the sampling exponent and the rest of the replay configuration in Table S2b.

Teacher configuration. Table S2 lists the architecture, optimization and world-model settings of the privileged teacher, every entry read from the deployed learner and worker code rather than from a training note.

Retrained CaRL teacher. CaRL [1] is retrained here with its own published recipe on the CrashCourse training split; no released checkpoint is evaluated in that row. We record the source repository and revision, deviations from the released implementation, privileged inputs and normalization, policy and value-network architecture, action parameterization, reward and termination rules, replay, training and update budgets, checkpoint selection, seeds, hardware and wall time. Run them on the same CrashCourse partitions, vehicle dynamics, timestep, control bounds and evaluator as MU-SAC. If a native method contract cannot be equalized without changing the method, preserve it and label the difference in the comparison rather than presenting the result as a controlled component ablation.

Teacher export schema. Each record contains dataset version; source clip/family/instance/split IDs; map, route, weather and physics hashes; teacher code/checkpoint/input contract; environment seed; timestamps and frame IDs; sensor paths, calibration and history masks; ego/navigation inputs; executed controls and delay; native future targets and validity masks; outcome, contact, departure and progress logs; selection decision and reason; collection/label-generation cost. Diagnostic trigger and hidden-state fields must be segregated from student inputs. Keep source-related variants in their declared partition and audit hashes for cross-split duplication. Report the rejected pool without training on failed actions as successful expert labels.

TFv6 adapter and DAgger schedule. Table S3 gives the executed configuration. The adapter is inserted on the 256-dimensional context-token sequence that TFv6's fused encoder hands to its planning decoder, is rank 16, and is gated: $W_{\rm up}$ is zero-initialized while the gate starts at one, so insertion is bit-identical to the released model at step zero and the gradient path through $W_{\rm up}$ is still live. Initializing both at zero would leave it dead. Every backbone parameter carries requires_grad false and an assertion fails at runtime if any decoder parameter is trainable, so the frozen-base claim is enforced rather than asserted. Before the full run, a 32-sample overfit check at $10^{-3}$ for 300 steps confirms that gradients reach the adapter and that the targets are learnable through it.

Demonstrations come from whichever privileged policy drives a given training instance perfectly; the pool is the reinforcement-learned teachers and the rule-based privileged controller, and an instance with no perfect demonstrator is not used. The first fit covered 29 such instances. Its held-out waypoint error was under a metre and its closed-loop behavior was worse than the untouched backbone on several encounters, including new collisions on ones the released model completed cleanly, which is the covariate-shift gap DAgger addresses. Three rounds follow, each initialized from the previous round's weights at the lower learning rate, each aggregating the states its own predecessor reached. The on-policy share of the training batch falls from 55% to 33% across the rounds as the accumulated corpus grows.

Native student training audit. For each selected release, record all sensor requirements, preprocessing, coordinate conventions, time horizon, controller/PID settings, native losses, auxiliary labels and causal history. Verify observation/target alignment on a visualized batch; check that incomplete history/future targets are masked. Run an initial-function equivalence check before SFT and confirm gradients only reach the adapter parameters. Freeze running statistics in base normalization layers. Report total/trainable parameters, placement/rank and initialization, optimizer, learning rate, batch size, steps, mixture, sampling policy, memory, wall time and validation rule. A small-batch fit check is a data/gradient sanity check, not evidence of closed-loop transfer.

Evaluation protocol details. Compute DS per route before averaging; do not multiply an aggregate RC by an aggregate penalty. Use the evaluator's collision accounting without a bespoke deduplication rule, and the pinned statistics manager's distance calculation. Use the actual scheduled route count for CrashCourse and the complete official manifest for general Bench2Drive; log infrastructure retries and resolve coverage before reporting final results. Record learner updates and wall time as well as environment steps, since additional critics change compute cost. Reimplementations of a competing teacher must be validated against that method's released code or reported reference setting. Count unique encounters, frames, sampled windows and optimization exposures separately, keep the total SFT update budget fixed across budgets, and account for teacher training, attempted collection, accepted-data storage, native label generation and SFT compute separately. Choose checkpoints on emergency development performance subject to a predeclared nominal-development constraint. In the common-onset protocol verify ego and actor state, adversary phase and controller state before the branch.

S3Analysis, Evidence Registry and Claim Gates

Episode-level results. Store one row per policy checkpoint, instance and environment seed, including split, group, DS, RC, SR, collision counts by type, evaluator distance, efficiency, comfort and retry status. Compute CR from pooled collisions and distance within each evaluation seed. Keep raw infractions and termination status for evaluator auditing. Preserve training seed separately from environment seed. Export seed-level and per-instance summaries and generate all tables, plots and prose numbers from the same versioned records. Report the four main metrics consistently in all comparisons.

Breakdown by scenario category. Tables S4 and S5 report the four main metrics for every policy within each of the six scenario dimensions of the test split. A route carries one label per dimension, so the two columns of a dimension partition the split while columns of different dimensions overlap; a column is therefore read against the other column of its own dimension and never across dimensions.

Paired uncertainty plan. Compute instance means across matched environment repetitions, then the declared all-instance aggregate for each training seed. Report training-seed mean and standard deviation. For uncertainty of adapted-minus-control differences, resample paired instance IDs as a shared cluster across arms and resample independent training-seed pairs as a second dimension; preserve all environment repetitions within each selected instance and retain a single original checkpoint when it is the reference. If split design requires source-family clustering, predeclare that cluster level and report the number of independent clusters. Specify confidence level, bootstrap repetitions and random seed before testing. Three training seeds yield limited precision; publish individual runs. General Bench2Drive regression reports only before/after DS and RC and their paired degradation.

Predeclared interpretation. A strong teacher and a gain on the adapted student support the intended teacher-transfer story for one named model, not for architectures in general. A strong teacher with poor transfer is an honest negative result, and a bottleneck is named only if evidence supports it. Minimal regression is a claim about the general Bench2Drive numbers alone. No table placeholder or favorable point estimate is itself evidence for any of these.
